Shift: 8 — START THE STRIKERS. DROP THE WINGER. PRESS HIGH AND FAST. NO MISTAKES TODAY.

The shift is 8 — every letter in the original was moved 8 places forward in the alphabet. To decrypt, you shift 8 places back. Caesar himself used a shift of 3. The principle is exactly the same.

How it works
1
Take the alphabet. Assign each letter a number: A=0, B=1, C=2 ... Z=25.
2
To encrypt the letter P with a shift of 8: P is position 15. Add 8. That's 23. Position 23 is X.
3
If you go past Z (position 25), wrap back round to A. So Z + 1 = A. This is called modular arithmetic — the same maths your phone uses to keep time.
Why is it weak? There are only 25 possible shifts. A computer can try all of them in a fraction of a second. Even by hand it's crackable in minutes — which is exactly what you just did.

How it works
1
Choose a keyword — say GOAL. Write it out repeatedly over your message: G O A L G O A L G O A L ...
2
Each letter of the keyword gives a different shift. G=6, O=14, A=0, L=11. The first letter of your message shifts by 6, the second by 14, the third by 0 (no change), the fourth by 11.
3
The letter E might become K in one place and S in another — because the shift changes. That breaks the frequency analysis that cracks Caesar.
Why was this so powerful? For nearly 300 years, from about 1550 to 1863, the Vigenère cipher was considered unbreakable. It was called le chiffre indéchiffrable — the indecipherable cipher. It was finally cracked by a Prussian army officer named Friedrich Kasiski, who found a pattern in repeated keyword sequences.

So what was that actually about?

What you just did — spotting patterns, testing hypotheses, working backwards from known structure — is exactly what cryptanalysts do. Cryptanalysis is a branch of computer science.

The ciphers you cracked are ancient. The principles behind them are not. They are the same ones that secure every message you have ever sent.

Go deeper

Cryptanalysis is the science of breaking codes without knowing the key. It sits at the intersection of mathematics, linguistics, and computer science — and it has shaped history more than almost any other discipline most people have never heard of.

The core skill is not memorising facts. It is noticing what should be there but isn't, or what appears too often to be random. In a Caesar cipher, the letter E — the most common letter in English — shows up shifted by the same amount every time. In a Vigenère cipher, repeated words produce repeated ciphertext at predictable intervals. In Enigma, German operators' habits leaked structure into what should have been noise.

This is called frequency analysis and known-plaintext attack — two of the oldest and most powerful tools in cryptanalysis. They work because humans are predictable, and because language has structure. Any cipher that fails to destroy that structure completely is vulnerable.

Modern encryption — AES, RSA, elliptic curve — is designed to produce output that is statistically indistinguishable from random noise, even if you know exactly how the algorithm works. That is what makes it hard. Not secrecy about the method, but mathematical complexity that makes brute force computationally impossible.

The field that studies all of this is called cryptology — it covers both cryptography (making codes) and cryptanalysis (breaking them). If this appeals to you, it sits firmly within computer science, mathematics, and information security.

GCHQ

GCHQ — Government Communications Headquarters — is the UK's signals intelligence and cyber agency. The kind of thinking you did today is exactly what they recruit for.

Their CyberFirst programme offers free courses for students aged 12–17, a university bursary, and a Degree Level Apprenticeship in Cyber Security. All run by the National Cyber Security Centre, part of GCHQ.[2][3]

They don't care where you went to school. They care how you think.

Go deeper

GCHQ was founded in 1919 as the Government Code and Cypher School — the same organisation that ran Bletchley Park. It was renamed GCHQ in 1946 and is now based in Cheltenham. Its mission covers signals intelligence, cybersecurity, and protecting UK government systems.

The National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC), which sits within GCHQ, was set up in 2016. When a major UK organisation gets hit by a cyberattack — a hospital, a government department, a bank — NCSC is typically involved in the response.

The CyberFirst programme exists because the UK has a significant shortage of people with the skills to work in this field. It is explicitly designed to reach students who might not think of themselves as "tech people." The courses cover cryptography, ethical hacking, networking, and digital forensics. No prior experience is required.

Beyond GCHQ, the same skills are in demand across defence, finance, healthcare, and technology. Cybersecurity is one of the fastest-growing employment sectors in the UK.

From Julius Caesar to your WhatsApp

Go deeper

Caesar cipher. Julius Caesar didn't invent substitution ciphers, but he used them systematically. His shift of 3 worked because most of his enemies were illiterate, and those who weren't had no concept of cryptanalysis. Security through obscurity — it works until it doesn't. The Roman historian Suetonius documented the cipher around 100 AD, which is why we know about it at all.

Vigenère cipher. The misattribution to Blaise de Vigenère is one of history's more persistent errors. Giovan Battista Bellaso published it in 1553. Vigenère published a different, stronger cipher in 1586 — but his name ended up attached to Bellaso's. Charles Babbage worked out how to crack it by 1854 but never published. One likely reason: he was serving as a cryptographic adviser to the British military during the Crimean War, and publishing would have told the enemy their codes were broken. Friedrich Kasiski published independently in 1863 and got the credit. The key insight: if the keyword repeats, so does the ciphertext pattern. Find the repeat, find the key length, and the cipher falls apart.

Enigma. The Enigma machine was commercially available in the 1920s — you could buy one. The German military made it significantly more complex. Crucially, the Poles cracked an earlier version in 1932 and shared their methods with British intelligence in 1939, weeks before Germany invaded Poland. Bletchley Park built on Polish foundations, not from scratch.

AES. Selected by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology in 2001 after an open international competition. The winning algorithm — Rijndael — was designed by two Belgian cryptographers, Joan Daemen and Vincent Rijmen. AES-256 uses a 256-bit key: 2²⁵⁶ possible keys. Even if every atom in the observable universe were a computer running since the Big Bang, you still wouldn't have checked them all. Quantum computers will eventually reduce that security margin, but AES-256 is still considered safe under realistic quantum threat models.

The Enigma connection

Enigma was a machine, not just a cipher. It used rotating wheels to create a shifting key that changed with every single keypress — over 103 sextillion possible configurations.

The team at Bletchley Park didn't crack it by being cleverer than the Germans. They found a flaw. German operators often started messages the same way — the same weather report, the same sign-off. That repetition gave the codebreakers a foothold. Sound familiar? It's the same trick you used in Round 1.

The lesson: no cipher is unbreakable. Every system has a weakness. Finding it is the job.
Go deeper

The Enigma machine worked through three (sometimes four) rotating wheels called rotors, a fixed reflector, and a plugboard. Each keypress advanced the rotors, so the same letter pressed twice produced two completely different outputs.

One critical design constraint: Enigma could never encrypt a letter as itself. Press A, and you would never get A back. This was considered a feature. It was a fatal weakness. It meant that if you knew a word somewhere in the message, you could rule out every position where that word's letters matched the ciphertext — dramatically narrowing the search.

This is a known-plaintext attack. German weather reports always contained "wetter." Daily messages often ended "Heil Hitler." These predictable phrases — called cribs — gave the codebreakers a lever.

Alan Turing designed an electromechanical machine called the Bombe to test thousands of possible Enigma configurations automatically. It didn't try every configuration — that would have been impossible. It used logical deduction to eliminate impossible ones and home in on plausible ones. At its peak, Bletchley Park was running over 200 Bombes and breaking up to 4,000 messages per day.[4]

The intelligence produced was classified under the codename Ultra. Its existence was kept secret until 1974.
